Synergy 2 — recency, confidence, and ways the score can mislead

This notebook uses the same reproducible offline results as Notebook 1. It compares half-lives 4, 6, 10, 20 and all-history weighting, several shrinkage strengths, improving/declining partnerships, lucky streaks, multiple-partner selection, rating errors and score-model errors. The final comparison uses a separate random seed after the candidate families were specified in code.

Recommendation to investigate, not ship blindly: a six-pair-game half-life is a reasonable product compromise. Use stronger small-sample caution than a simple games-played count suggests. Keep margin influence modest until its expected baseline is calibrated to real scoring formats.

1. Translating half-life into the group's sessions

At approximately 1.5 games together per session, a six-game half-life represents about four sessions. In a long history, the latest six games carry 50% of the weight, latest twelve 75%, latest eighteen 87.5%. Those shares are exact only as history tends to infinity; finite histories are normalized over observed games. This decay counts games with this specific partner, not calendar days.

The effective sample size is finite even with infinite history: geometric weights imply (1+lambda)/(1-lambda), lambda=2**(-1/h). This is an independence-based diagnostic, not a guarantee about real uncertainty.

2. Improving and declining partnerships

For 30 games the pair's true chance is 50%; then it becomes 70% (improving) or 30% (declining). The rating-based baseline is held at 50% to isolate the recency tradeoff. Updating ratings is tested later. Every setting is evaluated on the same histories. All examples below use 70% results and prior 6.

The population target in the table is the unshrunk current combined contribution; shrinkage deliberately biases the displayed estimate toward zero. RMSE to this target therefore penalizes both stale history and conservative shrinkage.

Shorter half-lives respond faster; longer half-lives are steadier when nothing has changed. 3. The strongest-looking partner can simply be lucky

All partners below have exactly zero chemistry and 50% expected/actual win probability. We select the highest-scoring partner out of 1, 8 or 16 candidates. A positive “best” score is therefore a selection artifact by construction.

This is not the false-positive rate of a calibrated statistical test: +15 is an illustrative UI score threshold, not a declared significance level. Rank selection alone always picks somebody, even when no partnership is special.

Prior 12 is worth testing as a more cautious default than prior 6. The tradeoff is smaller displayed scores for genuine improvement too. A long history does not remove all noise when old games are continually discounted.

Avoid hard claims such as “proven best partner.” Consider “strongest observed partnership” plus an explicit provisional label, separate games and win-rate context, and uncertainty validated under realistic repeated-session data. A minimum-games gate helps presentation but cannot alone fix selection bias.

4. Rating accuracy and persistent chemistry

A result residual estimates true chance - predicted chance. It cannot know whether the gap came from chemistry or an underestimated player. Baseline errors of ±15 probability points are tested with zero true chemistry.

A separate stress case gives a pair a persistent 70% true chance against a fixed-rating opponent. Both teammates' ratings receive the app's 40/20 Elo updates while the opponent rating is held fixed. This intentionally extreme fixed-pair schedule is not a rotating league: it isolates how individual Elo can absorb a partnership effect. Frozen and online baselines use the same outcomes within each half-life comparison.

This is the biggest semantic limitation. The proposed score measures recent performance beyond the current rating baseline, not an immutable or causal partnership trait. A permanently good pair may return toward zero once Elo expects them to keep winning. Fixing that requires a different baseline or a joint player-and-partnership model, not merely a different half-life.

The same +100 Elo latent partnership boost also produces different probability residuals for different opponent strengths. That is consistent with “beating the odds by percentage points,” but not a schedule-independent chemistry unit.

5. Margin-model failures and correlated sessions

First preserve every winner but make all games systematically closer or wider. A margin model trained on the original scoring style will assign nonzero chemistry even when win probabilities are perfectly calibrated. The candidate blend is also tested on games to 15 using an expected-margin mapping for 11.

Then add a shared session-form shock, with 1–4 games per session and mean block size 1.5. Shocks are symmetric on log odds, retaining a 50% marginal win chance. The spread of scores grows: treating all pair games as independent is optimistic.

7. Decision and what to do next

  1. Keep the shared signed residual concept. It passes the desired underdog examples and invariants, including team reversal and zero-centered nulls.
  2. Start product exploration with a six-game half-life. It represents about four sessions together and has a long-history effective sample size of only about seventeen independent games. Compare 4 and 10 if faster response or stability matters more; neither is universally correct.
  3. Treat 90% results / 10% calibrated margin, prior 12 as a cautious next candidate, alongside the original 70/30/prior-6 benchmark. More margin helps slightly in the self-consistent generator, but is vulnerable to score-model error. These weights are not a final production recommendation.
  4. Show win rate, games together and provisional evidence separately. Never interpret index points as win-probability percentage points.
  5. Decide whether persistent partnership ability should remain positive after individual ratings learn it. Current residuals cannot promise that behavior.
  6. Before implementation, use a separate synthetic rotating-player league with actual rating updates, then chronological validation on explicitly approved non-production match data. Fit margins separately by scoring format, assess opponent effects and group differences, and validate uncertainty before labeling any partner confidently strongest.

Limits: no real-user data, no app matchmaking integration, no serving-aware point engine, no inferred causal chemistry, no calibrated confidence intervals, and no production scoring changes. Pair histories are deliberately controlled experiments rather than a full league simulation. The six-game choice is a product compromise supported by tradeoffs, not proof of optimality.
